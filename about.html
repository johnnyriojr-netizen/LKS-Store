About myStore


Everything you wanted to know about your fave fashion brand. And then some.

The midday heat of Phnom Penh bounced off the asphalt of Russian Market, but inside the narrow doorway of **Krama & Cut**, the air was crisp, smelling faintly of lemongrass incense and fresh linen. At twenty-five, Sothea felt permanently caught between two worlds. By day, he drafted social media campaigns for a digital marketing agency off Norodom Boulevard; by night, he navigated his family’s gentle inquiries about when he’d settle down and take civil service exams. His quiet rebellion lived on the metal garment racks of the boutique he had opened six months ago with his friend Mey. The concept was simple yet defiant: everyday streetwear built from indigenous Cambodian textiles. Sothea didn't want the stiff, formal sampots reserved for temple mornings and wedding halls, nor did he want the cheap polyester fast fashion flooding weekend night markets. He wanted heavy drop-shoulder tees with raw silk pocket accents, relaxed linen overshirts dyed with natural indigo from Kampot, and tailored wide-leg trousers lined with discarded factory selvage denim. That Tuesday afternoon, the shop’s bell chimed, startling Sothea from his sketchpad. A young woman stood by the entrance, brushing rain mist from her denim jacket. She looked about Sothea's age, carrying a canvas tote bag plastered with local indie band pins and headphones draped around her neck. "Saw your Instagram reel about the handwoven chore coat," she said, scanning the minimalist racks. "Is the clay-dyed one still around?" "Last one left in a medium," Sothea said, stepping out from behind the counter. He pulled down a structured canvas jacket dyed a rich, earthy terracotta using river sediment from Battambang. "The weaver who spun the lining is actually from my mother’s home village. It softens up after a few wears, but the cut stays sharp." She slipped her arms through the sleeves, turned toward the floor-length mirror, and adjusted the lapels. The garment bridged the gap effortlessly—rooted in traditional craft, but cut clean enough for an air-conditioned coffee shop workspace or a rooftop gig in BKK1. "I've spent years looking for something like this," she murmured, turning to check the drape of the shoulders. "Everything else either feels like a costume or a carbon copy of something from abroad." "That was the whole idea," Sothea smiled, seeing the familiar spark he had chased for months. "You shouldn't have to choose between where you're from and who you are right now." When she paid and walked back out into the warm, sudden monsoon drizzle, Sothea watched her step into the vibrant, rumbling traffic of Toul Tompoung. He glanced back at his cutting table, where fresh reams of unbleached cotton waited, feeling that, for twenty-five, he was right where he belonged.
